# Actinic Keratosis / Intraepithelial Carcinoma (AKIEC)

## Overview
Actinic keratosis (AK) is a common precancerous skin lesion caused by
cumulative UV damage to keratinocytes. Bowen's disease (squamous cell
carcinoma in situ) is a closely related, slightly more advanced
intraepithelial lesion. Both are grouped here as AKIEC because they sit
on the same disease spectrum: untreated AK can progress to invasive
squamous cell carcinoma in a minority of cases, which is why dermatologists
treat them proactively.

## Typical Appearance
- Rough, dry, scaly patches or bumps, often easier to feel than see.
- Pink, red, or skin-colored, sometimes with a yellow-brown crust.
- Usually a few millimeters to about 1-2 cm, on chronically
  sun-exposed skin (face, scalp, ears, forearms, back of hands).
- Bowen's disease typically presents as a larger, well-demarcated,
  slow-growing red scaly patch that can resemble eczema or psoriasis.

## Risk Factors
- Cumulative lifetime UV exposure; fair skin, light eyes.
- Older age.
- Immunosuppression (e.g., organ transplant recipients).
- History of frequent sunburns or outdoor occupations.

## Diagnosis
Often diagnosed clinically by an experienced dermatologist; biopsy is
used when the diagnosis is uncertain or invasive squamous cell
carcinoma cannot be excluded (e.g., the lesion is thick, tender, or
rapidly growing).

## Treatment
- **Cryotherapy** (liquid nitrogen) for isolated lesions.
- **Topical field therapy** (5-fluorouracil, imiquimod, diclofenac gel)
  for multiple lesions or widespread sun damage ("field cancerization").
- **Photodynamic therapy** or **curettage** in select cases.
- Bowen's disease is often treated similarly to superficial BCC
  (excision, curettage, topical therapy, or photodynamic therapy).

## Prognosis & Follow-up
Most actinic keratoses do not progress to cancer, but the risk is not
zero, and it is impossible to predict clinically which lesions will
progress — hence the general recommendation to treat them. Regular
dermatologic follow-up and sun protection reduce the risk of new
lesions.

## When to Seek Care
New rough or scaly patches on sun-exposed skin, or a lesion that
becomes thickened, tender, or ulcerated, should be evaluated by a
dermatologist.
