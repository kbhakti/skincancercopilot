# Melanocytic Nevi (NV)

## Overview
Melanocytic nevi — commonly called moles — are benign proliferations of
melanocytes. Almost everyone has some nevi, typically acquired in
childhood and adolescence, with the total count often peaking in early
adulthood and gradually declining with age. The vast majority of nevi
remain stable and benign for life; a small minority of melanomas arise
within a pre-existing nevus, which is why monitoring for change matters.

## Typical Appearance
- Round or oval, symmetric, with a well-defined, regular border.
- Uniform color, ranging from tan/brown to dark brown or black
  depending on skin type; can be flat (junctional) or raised
  (compound/intradermal).
- Usually stable in size, shape, and color over long periods, though
  gradual, uniform changes over years (e.g., slight enlargement in
  childhood, gradual fading in older age) are normal.
- "Atypical" or dysplastic nevi may have somewhat irregular borders or
  color variation but still lack the more extreme ABCDE features seen
  in melanoma.

## Risk Factors for developing many nevi
- Genetic predisposition, fair skin.
- Sun exposure during childhood.
- Larger nevus count is itself a risk factor for melanoma (more
  melanocytes overall, and possible shared predisposing factors),
  which is why total mole count is part of melanoma risk assessment.

## Diagnosis
Diagnosis is typically clinical and dermoscopic. The key clinical task
is distinguishing a benign nevus from melanoma or an atypical nevus
that warrants biopsy — using the ABCDE rule and comparison against the
patient's other moles ("ugly duckling" sign).

## Treatment
No treatment is needed for typical benign nevi. Excisional biopsy is
recommended for any nevus that is changing, symptomatic, or clinically
atypical, both to treat and to obtain a definitive histopathological
diagnosis.

## Prognosis & Follow-up
Excellent for typical benign nevi. Patients with numerous or atypical
nevi, or a family history of melanoma, benefit from periodic
full-body skin exams and are often taught skin self-examination and
photographic mole-mapping to detect subtle change over time.

## When to Seek Care
Any mole that is changing in size, shape, or color, becomes symptomatic
(itching, bleeding, tenderness), or looks different from the patient's
other moles should be evaluated promptly.
