# Melanoma (MEL)

## Overview
Melanoma is a malignant tumor that arises from melanocytes, the
pigment-producing cells in the skin. It is less common than basal cell
carcinoma or squamous cell carcinoma but is responsible for the large
majority of skin-cancer deaths because it is more likely to invade
deeper tissue and metastasize to lymph nodes and distant organs if not
caught early. Early-stage melanoma is highly treatable; survival drops
substantially once the tumor becomes thick or has spread.

## Typical Appearance — the ABCDE rule
Dermatologists commonly screen pigmented lesions using the ABCDE
criteria. A lesion showing several of these features warrants prompt
evaluation:
- **A — Asymmetry:** one half of the lesion does not match the other.
- **B — Border:** edges are irregular, notched, or poorly defined.
- **C — Color:** uneven pigmentation with shades of brown, black, tan,
  red, white, or blue within the same lesion.
- **D — Diameter:** greater than about 6 mm (roughly the size of a
  pencil eraser), though melanomas can be smaller.
- **E — Evolving:** the lesion is changing in size, shape, color, or
  symptoms (itching, bleeding) over weeks to months.
The "ugly duckling" sign — a mole that looks different from a patient's
other moles — is another commonly used heuristic.

## Risk Factors
- Fair skin, light hair/eye color, tendency to sunburn rather than tan.
- History of significant UV exposure or blistering sunburns, especially
  in childhood, and use of tanning beds.
- Large number of common or atypical (dysplastic) moles.
- Personal or family history of melanoma.
- Immunosuppression.

## Diagnosis
Diagnosis begins with visual/dermoscopic examination. Any suspicious
lesion should undergo a biopsy (excisional biopsy is preferred when
feasible) for histopathological confirmation, which also determines
Breslow thickness — the single most important prognostic factor.
Sentinel lymph node biopsy may be recommended for thicker lesions to
check for spread.

## Treatment
- **Surgical excision** with margins based on tumor thickness is the
  primary treatment for localized melanoma.
- **Advanced/metastatic disease** may be treated with immunotherapy
  (e.g., checkpoint inhibitors), targeted therapy (for BRAF-mutated
  tumors), radiation, or chemotherapy, depending on staging and
  molecular profile.

## Prognosis & Follow-up
Prognosis correlates strongly with stage at diagnosis; thin, localized
melanomas have excellent outcomes with surgery alone, while thick or
metastatic disease carries a more guarded prognosis. Patients require
long-term dermatologic follow-up for recurrence and new primary
melanomas.

## When to Seek Care
Any new or changing pigmented lesion — particularly one meeting ABCDE
criteria or that looks different from a patient's other moles — should
be evaluated by a dermatologist promptly, ideally within days to a few
weeks, not months.
