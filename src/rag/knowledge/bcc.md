# Basal Cell Carcinoma (BCC)

## Overview
Basal cell carcinoma is the most common form of skin cancer. It
originates in the basal cells of the epidermis and is strongly
associated with cumulative sun exposure. BCC grows slowly and almost
never metastasizes, but if left untreated it can invade and destroy
surrounding skin, cartilage, and bone, so timely treatment is still
important.

## Typical Appearance
BCC can present in several forms:
- A pearly or waxy translucent bump, often with visible small blood
  vessels (telangiectasia), most common on sun-exposed areas like the
  face, ears, and neck.
- A flat, flesh-colored or brown scar-like lesion.
- A sore that bleeds, oozes, crusts, heals, and then returns —
  non-healing ulcers are a classic warning sign.
- A reddish, irritated patch that may itch.

## Risk Factors
- Cumulative UV exposure over a lifetime (occupational or recreational
  sun exposure, tanning bed use).
- Fair skin, light eyes, history of sunburns.
- Older age (though it can occur in younger adults with heavy sun
  exposure).
- Radiation exposure, chronic skin inflammation, or immunosuppression.
- History of a prior BCC or other skin cancer.

## Diagnosis
Diagnosis is confirmed by skin biopsy (shave or punch biopsy) with
histopathological examination. Dermoscopy can support the clinical
impression before biopsy.

## Treatment
Options depend on tumor size, location, and subtype, and include:
- **Surgical excision** or **Mohs micrographic surgery** (preferred for
  high-risk or cosmetically sensitive areas due to tissue-sparing,
  margin-controlled removal).
- **Curettage and electrodessication** for low-risk, superficial
  lesions.
- **Topical therapies** (e.g., imiquimod, 5-fluorouracil) or
  **photodynamic therapy** for select superficial BCCs.
- **Radiation therapy** for patients who are poor surgical candidates.
- **Targeted systemic therapy** (hedgehog pathway inhibitors) for
  advanced or metastatic (rare) disease.

## Prognosis & Follow-up
Cure rates are excellent (>95%) with appropriate treatment, especially
when caught early. Because patients with one BCC are at increased risk
of developing another, regular skin checks and sun protection are
recommended long-term.

## When to Seek Care
Any sore that does not heal within a few weeks, a new pearly bump, or a
changing patch on sun-exposed skin should be evaluated by a
dermatologist.
